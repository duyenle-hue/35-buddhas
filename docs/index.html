The Confession Buddhas

Thirty-Five Buddhas

For well over a thousand years, Buddhists in Vietnam, China and Tibet have recited these 35 names to let go of harm they have done.

You say each name, bow, and resolve to do better. The list comes from a Mahāyāna sutra, the chapter of the Ratnakūṭa in which the Buddha answers his disciple Upāli.

How to read the map

Shakyamuni, the historical Buddha, sits at the center. The other 34 surround him in all ten directions: the eight points of the compass, plus above and below. The arrangement says that awakening is present everywhere.

Numbers show the order of recitation. It circles from above, to east, round the compass, then below, three times over. Select any Buddha to see the painting, and use Next to follow the order.

Start from a habit

Each Buddha is traditionally linked to one kind of harm. Pick something you'd like to let go of.

How the practice works

Four steps of letting go

Tibetan teachers explain the practice through four "powers". Reciting the names is the remedy.

Regret

Honestly see a harm you caused, the way you'd regret swallowing something bad for you.

Refuge

Turn toward the Buddhas as a steady reference point, instead of toward guilt or excuses.

Remedy

Do something good to counter it. Here that means saying each name and bowing.

Resolve

Decide not to repeat the harm, for a day or for good, in a promise you can keep.

    Practice · bowing to the 35

    Bow to the 35 Buddhas

    This is the practice as people do it in temples and at home. You don't need to be Buddhist to try it.

    1. 1Sit or stand comfortably and take three slow breaths.
    2. 2Bring to mind one thing you said or did that you regret.
    3. 3For each Buddha, read the name aloud or silently, then bow or lower your head. Tap Bow to move on.
    4. 4At the end, resolve to do better and wish others well.

    Tip: press the space bar to bow.

    You've bowed to all thirty-five.

    The practice traditionally ends by dedicating its goodness to everyone. Take a breath, picture someone you care about, and someone you find difficult, and wish them both well.
